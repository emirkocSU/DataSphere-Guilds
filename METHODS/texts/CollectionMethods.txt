Vision & Imaging: AI’s Eyes on the World 🌍📷
AI’s hunger for visual data is immense – by 2025, image and video datasets accounted for over $1.1 billion of the AI training data market
scoop.market.us
. Computer vision applications need vast and varied images and videos, far beyond what exists today. Smartphones, cameras, and even drones are key tools for gathering this visual goldmine. Contributors can collect new images/video or label existing ones, expanding AI’s “eyes” to see every corner of the world.
Street-Level & Map Imagery (Geo-Photos): Using smartphone cameras or car dashcams, individuals capture photos/videos of roads, street signs, storefronts, and landmarks in their area. These images, especially when GPS-tagged, fill mapping gaps and train navigation AIs (for example, recognizing new traffic signs or road changes). Collection: Smartphone camera, dashcam; Labeling: Annotate road features, sign types, or points of interest on maps. This meets the booming demand from mapping services and autonomous driving systems (the automotive sector alone takes up ~22% of AI’s training data needs
scoop.market.us
).
Indoor Scene & Object Images: Photos of home or office environments, appliances, furniture, and everyday objects taken with phone cameras. These help AI understand indoor scenes for applications like augmented reality (AR) and domestic robots. Collection: Smartphone camera (optionally with device depth sensors for 3D data); Labeling: Tag objects in the scene or draw bounding boxes (e.g. outline a chair, TV, or refrigerator). As home automation grows, so does demand for such data to train cleaning robots and AR apps to recognize all the things in our daily life.
Retail Shelf & Product Display Photos: Contributors acting as “mystery shoppers” photograph supermarket shelves or product displays with their phones. These images train AI in retail analytics – e.g. detecting out-of-stock items or product placements. Collection: Smartphone camera (often with an app guiding angles); Labeling: Mark product categories, count items, or flag gaps on shelves. Retail and e-commerce companies crave this data to improve inventory management (retail AI usage is rising as part of the 7% share of AI data in commerce
scoop.market.us
).
General Object Photography (Diverse Objects & Backgrounds): High-demand objects (from common tools to rare plant species) photographed in various settings. This enriches object recognition models beyond the typical internet images. Collection: Smartphone or DSLR camera; Labeling: Provide the object name/category (“this is a wrench”) and possibly context tags (“outdoors”, “on table”). By allowing users worldwide to contribute, the dataset gains diversity (different cultures, environments) which reduces bias and improves robustness

.
Image Annotation Tasks (Tagging and Bounding Boxes): Instead of collecting new photos, users can label existing images from datasets. This includes drawing bounding boxes around objects, segmenting areas (e.g. delineating a tree in an image), or tagging attributes (like identifying clothing styles or counting people). Collection: (Data provided by platform or clients); Labeling: Detailed annotation via browser or mobile app tools. These tasks provide massive employment opportunities – e.g. a single image dataset can involve annotating 650,000+ objects in 185,000 images

 for autonomous vehicles or facial recognition. Human labelers remain critical even as AI assists, because human judgment ensures quality and edge-case handling.
Image Captioning (Vision-to-Language): Looking at an image and writing a descriptive caption or identifying what's happening. This multimodal task pairs visual data with text, training AI to connect sight and language (used in photo search or for assisting the visually impaired). Collection: Existing images (or user’s own photos); Labeling: Freelancers write one-line or paragraph descriptions for each image. For example, an image of a park might be captioned “A child on a red bike rides along a sunny park path.” Such richly labeled data is in high demand for next-gen multimodal AI

Video Clips & Activity Recognition: Short videos recorded on smartphones, GoPros, or wearable cameras capturing activities (sports, cooking, daily tasks). Video is a step beyond images – it’s needed for action recognition (teaching AI to understand motion and events). Collection: Smartphone or action camera (users film various actions or scenarios, e.g. someone performing a yoga pose or a car driving by); Labeling: Annotate the start/end of key actions in the video or classify the activity (“pouring coffee”, “jumping jack exercise”). With the rise of robotics and autonomous drones, demand for labeled video data has exploded. Large-scale video datasets (like Kinetics-700 with hundreds of thousands of clips) are used to train models, but still more diverse clips are required to cover endless real-world possibilities.

Audio & Speech: AI’s Ears and Voice 🔊🗣️
From voice assistants to automated transcription, AI that hears and speaks relies on vast amounts of audio data. By 2025, audio datasets were nearly on par with vision in market share (about $1.0+ billion of the training data market)
scoop.market.us
, and this demand keeps growing. Contributors can record new sounds or annotate audio clips – microphones in smartphones and laptops are key sensors here. Many languages and sound environments remain underrepresented, creating a huge opportunity for crowdsourced audio data collection.
Spoken Command Recordings: Users record themselves saying common voice assistant commands or queries (e.g. “What’s the weather like tomorrow?” in various languages). These clips teach virtual assistants (Alexa, Siri, etc.) to understand natural speech patterns. Collection: Smartphone microphone (often via an app that prompts with phrases); Labeling: The phrase is auto-transcribed, and contributors verify the text or annotate any misheard words. This data is in high demand as voice UIs spread – companies need diverse accents and languages so the AI works for everyone (today’s models still struggle with accented or non-English speech, a gap this data helps fill).
Conversational Dialogue Audio: Pairs or groups of people record unscripted conversations or read dialogue scripts, creating realistic speech interaction data. Collection: Microphone (phone or PC) – two people might use an app to chat and record, even if remote; Labeling: Transcribe the conversation into text, and sometimes label speaker turns or intentions. Such dialogue datasets train customer service bots and chatbots to handle natural, back-and-forth speech. With the boom in AI customer support, high-quality conversational audio is extremely valuable (including informal speech, slang, and varied accents which are often missing in corporate datasets).
Low-Resource Language Speech: Native speakers of less-represented languages (or dialects) record audio in their tongue – reading passages, speaking spontaneously, or translating from another language. Collection: Smartphone mic or dedicated recording devices; Labeling: Transcriptions and translations (often provided by the speaker or a second annotator). This addresses a major global need: most speech recognition systems only excel in a few languages, leaving a gap for billions of people. By contributing, say, Swahili or Turkish dialect speech, users help build voice AI for their communities. Demand is huge – there’s a push to expand ASR (Automatic Speech Recognition) to hundreds of languages, and crowdsourced audio is the only scalable way to get there.
Environmental Sound Captures: Audio recordings of ambient environments and distinct sounds. For example, users might record city street noise, nature sounds like rainstorms or birds, appliances running, car engine sounds, etc. Collection: Smartphone or portable recorder (possibly with apps that sample sound levels); Labeling: Each clip is labeled with the sound event(s) (“car honking”, “dogs barking”, “restaurant crowd noise”). These data fuel sound recognition AI – important for smart home devices (recognizing alarms or glass breaks), urban planning (mapping noise pollution), and multimedia search. There’s high demand for diverse environmental audio because sounds vary greatly by location and context (different cultures have different daily soundscapes).
Speech-to-Text Transcription Tasks: Instead of providing new audio, contributors can label existing audio data by transcribing it. Platforms often have large collections of phone call recordings, podcasts, or videos that need accurate transcripts for NLP training. Collection: (Audio provided); Labeling: Listen via headset and type out what is said, marking pauses or speaker changes as needed. Despite advances in automatic speech recognition, human transcribers are needed to achieve near-perfect accuracy and to handle difficult audio (heavy accents, background noise). High-quality transcripts serve as ground truth to train and evaluate speech AI. With audio dataset revenues hitting $1.01B in 2025
scoop.market.us
, transcription work remains plentiful.
Audio Annotation & Metadata Tagging: Beyond transcribing words, some tasks involve labeling audio characteristics – e.g. marking speaker identity in a multi-speaker clip, noting emotions in someone’s voice, or tagging audio with its language. Collection: (Audio provided, or user’s own recordings); Labeling: Use annotation tools to highlight sections (like “speaker A from 0-10s, speaker B from 10-20s”) or choose descriptors (emotion: happy/sad, audio quality: clear/muffled). These labels help train speaker recognition systems and emotion-detection AI (used in call centers, healthcare, etc.). For instance, a company might need thousands of clips labeled for whether the speaker sounds frustrated to train an AI that alerts human agents in real time. It’s nuanced work requiring human perception – an area where automation still falls short, meaning strong ongoing demand for human annotators.
(Why it’s high-demand: Voice-enabled AI is expanding globally – every new language, accent, or acoustic scenario needs data. Tech giants and startups alike race to improve speech recognition and generation, fueling a data collection spree. One translation service needed 300,000+ recordings from non-native speakers to improve its speech models
scoop.market.us
, and voice assistants continually seek fresh command data to handle new slang or noise conditions. In short, AI’s ears always need more training to truly understand the world of sound.)
Text & Language: AI’s Knowledge Base 📖✍️
AI systems like chatbots and translators thrive on textual data – from websites, books, dialogs, to enterprise documents – but they need human guidance to ensure quality. Text datasets remain the largest segment of training data (text contributed ~$1.3 billion in 2025, the single biggest share)
scoop.market.us
. There is a huge gap in well-labeled, domain-specific text for AI. DataSphere Guilds enables people to create and curate the written word: whether simple labeling of tweets or advanced tasks like summarizing legal docs. All contributions help feed the ever-hungry language models while giving contributors control over data they generate or annotate.
Content Annotation (Tags, Categories, Sentiment): Workers read pieces of text – tweets, product reviews, news articles, forum posts – and assign labels or categories. Collection: (Text provided via the platform from public or partner sources); Labeling: Tag each text with things like sentiment (positive/negative/neutral), topic category (“sports”, “politics”), or flags (offensive or not). This kind of labeling is fundamental for content recommendation systems, sentiment analysis, and content moderation filters. For example, a social media company might crowdsource labeling of 9,000+ comments to identify toxic language
scoop.market.us
. The demand for such moderation and classification is massive and growing alongside online content volumes.
Named Entity Recognition & Data Extraction: In documents or sentences, contributors identify and mark specific information – e.g. highlight all person names, companies, dates, or product names. They might also extract structured data like filling a form from an unstructured text (for instance, reading a job posting and extracting “job title”, “salary”, etc.). Collection: (Texts provided, ranging from news to PDFs); Labeling: Use a text highlight tool to mark entities or copy information into fields. AI models use this to learn how to parse information from text (think digital assistants pulling facts from articles, or financial AI scanning news for company names). Because accuracy is crucial (a mis-labeled entity can mislead an AI), human verification remains key – and with enterprises drowning in text data, these annotation tasks provide steady work.
Translation Pair Creation (Bilingual Data): Bilingual contributors translate sentences or paragraphs from one language to another, or verify/correct machine translations. Collection: Source text provided (could be English sentences to translate into Spanish, etc., or vice versa); Labeling: The user produces an accurate translation, maintaining meaning and tone. This yields parallel sentence pairs for training translation models. Even with modern translation systems, high-quality human-translated data is pure gold – especially for languages where machine translations still err. By expanding coverage to more language pairs and informal speech, such tasks attack a huge demand gap in AI. (Many languages have scant translation data, so every contributed sentence helps bridge AI’s multilingual understanding).
Text Summarization & Writing Tasks: Users read a long text (news article, blog, research paper) and write a concise summary or explain it in simpler terms. Another variant is writing questions & answers based on a text, or creating brief bullet point notes. Collection: Source text provided; Labeling: Free-form text output by the contributor (summary, Q&A, etc.), often with quality checks. These tasks supply training data for generative AI – models that summarize or answer questions (like GPT-based systems). As companies deploy AI writers and analysts, they need high-quality human-crafted summaries as examples. Domain experts might summarize a medical report, while everyday users might summarize general news. The knowledge and context humans provide in these summaries help AI learn to focus on important points and to communicate clearly.
OCR and Handwriting Transcription: Contributors help convert scanned images of text into digital text. For instance, a photo of a handwritten form or an old printed document is given, and the worker types out the content. Collection: Images of text provided (or in some cases, users can scan documents with their phone); Labeling: Manual transcription of all text in the image, including notes about any illegible parts. This data is crucial for training Optical Character Recognition (OCR) models, especially for difficult cases like cursive handwriting or noisy images. There is a big demand for digitizing archives, receipts, and handwritten notes via AI – but first, AI needs to see lots of examples of human-transcribed text to learn from. By contributing to these tasks, individuals aid in building models that one day automate form processing (and yet ironically, it’s the human-generated ground truth that makes it possible!).
AI Prompt & Response Evaluation (RLHF): A more advanced text task: users work with AI-generated responses – ranking them, correcting them, or writing better ones. This is known as Reinforcement Learning from Human Feedback (RLHF), used to align AI systems with human preferences and ethical guidelines. Collection: AI outputs provided (e.g. a chatbot’s answer to a question); Labeling: The human rates the quality, flags issues (like if the answer is factually wrong or inappropriate), or provides an improved answer. With generative AI being deployed everywhere, this feedback data is in tremendous demand to fine-tune models. It’s a hybrid human-AI workflow: the AI generates content, humans label/judge it. DataSphere Guilds will include such ethical auditing tasks in which people ensure the AI’s language is appropriate, unbiased, and useful – a service critical enough that ethics and transparency are now coming to the forefront of AI development

.
(Why it’s high-demand: Language is how AI interfaces with knowledge and with us – but raw text off the internet isn’t enough. Models need curated, labeled examples to truly grasp sentiment, context, and correctness. For instance, a chatbot training set might include 200,000 human-curated questions and 2,000,000 answers
scoop.market.us
 to cover a wide range of queries. Every new domain (legal, medical, technical) requires experts to label and explain text so AI can learn it. With global AI usage booming in enterprise (finance, law, government), the gap between raw data and AI-ready data is widening – creating countless opportunities for people to contribute through text annotation, translation, and knowledge distillation.)
Geospatial & Environmental Data: Mapping the World 📍🌐
AI doesn’t just need to see and hear – it needs to understand our world in a spatial and environmental context. Geospatial data (maps, GPS traces, satellite imagery) and environmental sensor data (weather, climate, pollution) are in high demand to power smart cities, navigation, climate models, and location-based services. Many regions still lack detailed data, and AI models suffer from blind spots. By leveraging smartphones, IoT sensors, and drones, everyday people can collect data that literally maps the globe. These projects are often hybrid human-automation efforts: sensors gather raw data and humans annotate or validate it. The result is a richer collective dataset for AI – assembled from the ground up by the crowd.
GPS Tracks and Route Mapping: Users record their travel paths using phone GPS (e.g. walking, biking, driving routes). By sharing these traces, they contribute to live maps and traffic models (similar to how Google Maps or Waze improve with user data). Collection: Smartphone GPS (via an app that logs location periodically); Labeling: Minimal labeling – sometimes users can annotate the purpose (“commute to work” vs “scenic hike”) or flag points of interest along the way. Aggregating many users’ traces helps map algorithms learn new roads, trail paths, and typical traffic speeds. This is crucial in lesser-mapped areas globally. Indoor mapping is another frontier: users can also collect location data inside large buildings (using Wi-Fi/Bluetooth signals or phone sensors) to map malls, airports, etc., addressing a data gap in indoor navigation.
Local Street and Infrastructure Reports: Beyond just photos, contributors can actively survey their environment for issues or changes. For example, report new construction, road closures, potholes, or the presence of wheelchair ramps and bike lanes. Collection: Smartphone (geo-tagged photos or form inputs in an app); Labeling: Fill structured reports (type of issue, severity) or verify if a predicted issue (from AI or public data) is truly there. This human validation makes map data fresh and accurate. Municipalities and map providers have a strong need for up-to-date local data that only residents can supply in real time. It’s an opportunity for citizens to “work for AI” by improving their own community’s data while getting paid.
Satellite Imagery Annotation (Overhead Vision): Users with some training can help label satellite or aerial images, which are captured automatically but require human interpretation. Tasks include drawing polygons around buildings or fields, classifying land use (residential vs industrial), or spotting features like swimming pools or solar panels from above. Collection: Imagery is provided by satellites/drones; Labeling: Web-based map annotation tools (outline objects, assign category labels). This data trains geospatial AI to analyze Earth from above – useful for urban planning, agriculture monitoring, and disaster response (e.g. identifying damaged buildings after a hurricane). There is huge unmet demand here: governments and NGOs have more imagery than analysts to label it, and AI models need diverse training to generalize to different regions and seasons. Crowdsourcing these labels accelerates projects like identifying every house in a country or mapping crops for food supply monitoring.
Weather and Climate Crowd Data: Individuals can contribute local environmental readings to supplement official weather data. For example, a user’s smartphone barometer can upload pressure readings, aiding hyper-local weather forecasting (phones have been used to improve weather models by crowd-reading pressure). Others with home weather stations can share temperature, humidity, and rainfall data. Collection: Smartphone sensors (barometer, ambient temperature sensor) or dedicated IoT devices; Labeling: Automatic in many cases (device provides numeric data). Humans may add context like “raining” vs “clear” if sensors are limited. As climate change drives volatile weather, fine-grained data is extremely valuable – it helps train AI to predict flash floods, urban heat islands, or air quality on a neighborhood level. The DataSphere platform can integrate smart home devices and wearables as data sources, tapping into 40+ sensor types (e.g. thermometers, air quality sensors) already in use.
Noise & Pollution Mapping: Using phone microphones or inexpensive sensors, users record noise levels or pollution (air quality, light pollution) at different places and times. Collection: Smartphone mic (for noise in decibels) or connected sensors (for PM2.5 air quality, light meters, etc.); Labeling: Upload readings with location/time, optionally annotate source (“traffic”, “construction noise”). City planners and environmental agencies need this granular data to train AI that identifies pollution hotspots and recommends mitigations. For example, an AI might learn to predict noise complaints if it’s trained on crowdsourced noise maps. This empowers citizens to directly influence environmental improvements through data sharing.
Agriculture & Nature Observations: Farmers, gardeners, or nature enthusiasts gather data from the natural world. Examples: a farmer uses a drone to capture aerial images of crops weekly to monitor health; a gardener takes photos of plant diseases on leaves; hikers record sightings of wildlife or invasive species via an app (with GPS). Collection: Drones with cameras, smartphone cameras, possibly soil sensors or IoT farm sensors (moisture, pH); Labeling: annotate images (e.g. outline areas of pest damage on a crop image, or tag the species of an animal in a photo). Agricultural AI needs data on crops at all growth stages and conditions to train models for yield prediction or pest detection. There’s a large demand in agritech for labeled images of crops with and without disease, which far outstrips the data available today. Similarly, ecological AI projects seek wildlife data from around the globe – something only a crowd of nature observers can provide at scale. Contributors with domain knowledge (like agronomists or bird watchers) add immense value here, but even casual contributors can help by collecting and labeling what they see in nature.
(Why it’s high-demand: As the world changes, spatial data gets outdated quickly – AI needs constant streams of fresh data to stay relevant in navigation, climate, and environmental monitoring. Public sector AI projects (smart cities, disaster management) rely on such data, and the government sector constitutes about 12% of AI’s data usage
scoop.market.us
, a share likely to grow. Traditional mapping and meteorology can’t be everywhere at once, but millions of smartphones and IoT sensors globally can. By organizing these contributions, we fill critical gaps – from mapping rural roads to training climate models with on-the-ground sensor inputs – ensuring AI’s view of the world is complete and up-to-date.)
Autonomous Vehicles & Robotics: Navigating the Future 🚗🤖
Self-driving cars, delivery drones, and robots of all kinds are data-hungry monsters – they require an incredibly rich sensor understanding to operate safely in the real world. The autonomous vehicles industry already accounts for 22% of AI training data demand
scoop.market.us
, and this will only increase as companies race toward driverless technology. Likewise, household and industrial robots need diverse training data to handle varied environments. While these machines have sensors (cameras, LiDAR, radar) collecting raw data, human input is essential to label and interpret that data for training. DataSphere Guilds mobilizes people to accelerate this: by contributing driving footage, sensor logs, and meticulous annotations, we help teach robots to perceive and decide – a field with an acute demand gap and high growth potential rather than any decline from automation.
Driving Video Collection & Annotation: Individuals can use dashcams or smartphone cameras while driving to capture road scenes around the world – from highways to city streets. These videos (and snapshots from them) feed into training self-driving AI, especially for regions or scenarios where companies have little data. Collection: Car-mounted cameras or phones (with user consent and privacy safeguards like blurring license plates/faces); Labeling: Frame-by-frame annotation of key objects – other vehicles, pedestrians, bicycles, traffic signs, lane markings, etc. Labelers draw boxes or segment shapes on each object and sometimes add descriptions (e.g. “emergency vehicle” or “school zone sign”). This is labor-intensive but invaluable: one autonomous vehicle project reported labeling hundreds of thousands of images with over 600,000 object annotations to reach human-level perception
scoop.market.us
. Crowdsourced efforts can target critical gaps (night-time driving scenes, rare events like animals on the road) where additional data is desperately needed to make AI safer.
LiDAR and 3D Sensor Data Annotation: Modern cars and drones use LiDAR (laser scanners) and radar to sense depth – resulting in 3D point clouds or radar signal data. Annotating this data is a specialized but high-demand task. Contributors (often those with some technical skill) view 3D point cloud visualizations and label objects within them, such as drawing 3D bounding boxes around cars and pedestrians in a LiDAR scan. Collection: Typically, raw data comes from vehicles’ sensors (some tech enthusiasts with LiDAR-equipped drones or cars could also contribute raw scans); Labeling: Use specialized 3D annotation tools to mark and classify each object cluster in the point cloud. This training data is critical for autonomous navigation – AI must learn to detect obstacles in 3D, not just in images. The need for skilled human annotators here is intense – autonomous driving companies have hired armies of labelers, yet still can’t get enough diverse training scenarios (e.g. unusual weather conditions in LiDAR scans). DataSphere Guilds would allow distributed labeling of 3D data, possibly leveraging semi-automation to handle the massive volume while humans verify edge cases.
Robot Navigation in Indoor Spaces (SLAM Data): For indoor robots (like delivery bots, vacuum cleaners, or AR headsets mapping a room), crowd-collected mapping data can jumpstart their navigation algorithms. Users might walk through buildings with their smartphone, using AR apps to scan the environment (using the phone camera + IMU sensors) and create 3D maps of interiors. Collection: Smartphone cameras with depth sensing (or even consumer gadgets like iRobot mapping data shared by users); Labeling: Annotate the resulting map with semantic labels if needed (e.g. mark “kitchen”, “stairs” on a floor plan, or label obstacles and room features). This contributes to SLAM (Simultaneous Localization and Mapping) datasets that are otherwise hard to obtain at scale. As service robots become more common, there’s growing demand for data representing homes and offices – layouts differ hugely across the globe, so global crowd input is the best way to get representative training data. Importantly, any personally sensitive data (like identifiable interiors) can be handled with privacy filters (blurring family pictures, etc.) so that useful structural data is collected without violating privacy.
Drone Imagery for Inspection and Delivery: Drone pilots (amateur or professional) can capture video or imagery of landscapes, infrastructure, and urban scenes from the sky. These unique viewpoints train AI systems in drone navigation and environmental monitoring. For example, a contributor might fly a drone over farmland to record crop conditions, or over power lines to record infrastructure. Collection: Camera drones (DJI etc.) with GPS tagging; Labeling: Depending on project – could be annotating the footage for objects (e.g. mark the location of any people or animals in a field, for safety algorithms) or labeling conditions (identify areas of crop stress, or rust on equipment in footage). Automated delivery drones also need to recognize landing zones and obstacles from above – something that can be taught by having humans label rooftop and yard images for “safe to land” vs “hazard”. The appetite for diverse aerial data is growing as drones see wider use. By enabling enthusiasts worldwide to share and label their drone captures, we crowdsolve the data scarcity in this niche.
Vehicle Telematics & Driving Behavior Data: Modern vehicles output a stream of sensor data (speed, acceleration, brake pressure, engine diagnostics). If drivers opt in via a connected OBD-II device or a smartphone app, they can share anonymized driving telemetry. These time-series data, especially when combined with context (e.g. weather, road type), help train AI for predictive maintenance (detecting when a car might fail) or even driver-assist systems that detect aggressive driving. Collection: OBD-II sensor dongles or smartphone apps connected to the car; Labeling: Often automated (record numeric sensor logs). Humans might later annotate events of interest (“hard braking here due to deer crossing”) to give context. This kind of data is valuable for automotive safety research and insurance tech (usage-based insurance models). It’s an example of hybrid human-AI data gathering: sensors collect raw numbers, and humans supply interpretation for edge cases. Legal compliance is critical here – DataSphere would ensure any personal data (like exact routes or VIN numbers) are stripped, focusing only on the functional data patterns, to respect privacy while still leveraging the wisdom of crowd-driving.
(Why it’s high-demand: Autonomy is the frontier of AI, and it lives or dies by data. Self-driving cars have logged millions of miles, yet they still encounter surprises – the long tail of rare conditions demands ever more training data. Every new city a robotaxi expands to requires localization data and new training on local visuals (hence the insatiable need for crowdsourced local driving footage and map data). Robotics in homes and warehouses similarly need varied floor plans and scenarios. The bottom line: these industries aren’t slowing down – if anything, they need more humans in the loop supplying data to get to full automation safely. By contributing to this category, individuals become part of the development of groundbreaking technologies, all while filling the massive demand gap for annotated sensor data.)

Healthcare is a domain with arguably the greatest potential benefit from AI – and also some of the greatest data challenges. Medical AI models (for diagnosis, drug discovery, patient monitoring) need vast amounts of high-quality, diverse health data, but such data is hard to come by due to privacy, regulations, and rarity of expert labels. The result is an enormous unmet demand: AI in medicine is hungry for images, sensor readings, and patient data that truly represent global populations

. DataSphere Guilds aims to include ethical and compliant ways for individuals (including medical professionals and patients themselves) to contribute to healthcare AI datasets, with proper consent and anonymization. This way, people can help advance medical AI – and get paid for it – without breaching privacy. Key sensors include medical imaging devices, wearables, and health apps, often with specialists providing labels.
Medical Imaging Annotation (X-rays, MRIs, Ultrasound): Certified radiologists or trained annotators examine medical images to label important features – e.g. marking tumors on an MRI, classifying an X-ray as normal or showing pneumonia. Collection: Images come from healthcare partners or open datasets (patients’ identities removed); Labeling: Drawing regions of interest (outlining a lesion) or assigning diagnosis labels to images. This is critical for developing AI that can assist in diagnostics. The demand gap is huge – for many diseases, there are not enough example images for AI to learn from, and acquiring labels is slow because it requires experts. By creating a channel for radiology professionals worldwide to contribute in their spare time, DataSphere could dramatically scale up medical image datasets. (For instance, an AI model might need thousands of labeled MRI scans of a rare condition – something achievable only if many experts collaboratively contribute cases from different hospitals in a privacy-safe way.)
Pathology and Microscopy Data: Similar to radiology, but at a cellular level – e.g. labeling digitized pathology slides (microscope images of biopsies) to mark cancerous cells, or annotating blood smear images for cell types. Collection: High-resolution slide scans provided by labs (anonymized); Labeling: Specialized tools to mark cell boundaries or count abnormal cells. AI in pathology can greatly speed up lab work, but again, it needs large annotated datasets for training. Engaging a global pool of medical lab technicians via crowdsourcing can accelerate data preparation that would otherwise take years in research settings. (This requires niche knowledge, so it’s a good example of intermediate/advanced domain work that DataSphere Guilds supports alongside everyday tasks.)
Wearable & Fitness Sensor Data: Users contribute data from their smartwatches, fitness bands, or smartphone health apps – such as heart rate logs, step counts, workout records, sleep tracker data, etc. When aggregated (with identities removed), this data teaches AI about human health patterns in daily life. Collection: Wearables (Apple Watch, Fitbit, etc.) exported data or via API integration; Smartphone sensors like accelerometer for steps, GPS for runs; Labeling: Users may annotate events (“felt dizzy at 2pm”, “meditated here”) or simply share the raw labeled by context (e.g. the app knows it was a “30-minute run”).
